Pet nutrition myths every owner should stop believing

PodVet Editorial · September 2026

AK

Dr. Ayesha Khan, DVM

Nutrition & Wellness Consultant · Sep 5, 2026

Feeding advice for pets changes fast, and a lot of what "everyone knows" is marketing or folklore. Here are the nutrition myths we hear most often — and what the evidence actually says.

Myth 1: "Grain-free is always better"

Grains are not fillers. Quality whole grains provide energy, fibre and nutrients, and true grain allergies in dogs and cats are rare. The bigger story with grain-free diets is that swapping grains for legumes and pulses — done carelessly — has been linked to heart problems in some dogs. Unless your vet specifically recommends grain-free, a complete diet with grains is very often the better (and cheaper) choice.

Myth 2: "Raw is natural, so it must be healthy"

"Natural" is not a guarantee of safety. Raw meat carries bacteria like Salmonella and Listeria that can make both pets and the people handling their food ill, and home-prepared raw diets are frequently unbalanced over time. If you choose to feed raw, use a vet-formulated recipe and handle it like raw chicken you'd prepare for yourself.

Myth 3: "Human food is toxic to pets"

The blanket version is wrong. Plain cooked meat, rice, carrots, green beans and eggs are fine as occasional treats. What's genuinely toxic is specific things: chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, xylitol (in sugar-free gum), and raw dough. Plain, whole foods in sensible amounts are safe — the "all human food" panic is itself a myth.

Myth 4: "A clean bowl means they're not overeating"

Many pets eat whatever is in front of them and will finish a "full" bowl regardless. Body condition — the ribs you can feel but barely see — is the honest signal. Weigh food by cup or gram as recommended on the label, and adjust based on the pet's actual condition, not the bowl.

Clinic tip

Keeping a body condition score (BCS) in PodVet at every check-up turns a one-off guess into a trend. Two visits later you can show the owner the numbers moving the right way — easier than a lecture, and it builds trust.

The takeaway

Ask your vet before changing your pet's diet, trust "complete and balanced" labels from reputable brands, and judge success by body condition and energy — not by marketing stickers on the bag.
